    Limitations¶

    This page has two parts. The first says where the tool is weak; the second covers points that are not weaknesses of the tool but that you need to know when interpreting the results.

    Limitations of the tool¶

    The sentence thresholds were calibrated on newspaper columns only¶

    The short_sent_ratio and long_sent_ratio thresholds (TR 4/17, EN 8/32) were derived from the 15th and 85th percentiles of the sentence-length distribution in newspaper columns, measured with the default sentence rule and the default word:

    Language Columnists Sentences
    Turkish 137 198,225
    English 30 52,745

    A column is a single genre. Sentence length also varies with genre, so the thresholds may come out differently for novels, technical writing, transcripts, poetry or children's books. Both corpora come from a limited number of writers; in the English corpus of only 30 columnists in particular, the thresholds may reflect those writers' style. The thresholds show where a sentence falls in the distribution; they do not show whether readers actually find such sentences short or long. Method: Threshold calibration.

    Constants taken from sources were not tested on Turkish¶

    MTLD's 0.72 threshold, HD-D's 42-word sample and the 0.172 of Brunet's measure were taken from their sources and have not been tested on Turkish texts. It cannot be said that they do not carry over to Turkish, but it has not been shown that they do. Details: Change the thresholds.

    Thirteen citations are secondary¶

    13 citations (12 in Turkish) carry as cited in: the primary source could not be obtained and the formula was taken from the citing work. For example:

    Herdan (1960/1964), as cited in Tweedie & Baayen (1998) p.327, eq. (5)

    Affected measures include herdan_c, herdan_vm, honore_r, maas_a2, dugast_u, simpson_d, heaps_beta, lix, cttr and summer_s. The formulas were verified, but not against the primary source's own wording.

    Accuracy of the language model¶

    POS tags, morphological tags, English lemmas and the dependency features come from the spaCy model. Change the model and those numbers change. Turkish lemmas come from Zeyrek (below).

    Altinok (2023, p.13747), who developed the Turkish model, reports that on their own test data the model tags parts of speech correctly 90% of the time, falling to 63% for dependencies when the relation label is included. The model was trained on formal written sources; accuracy may differ on other kinds of text. Interpret the features that rest on this information with care, especially the dependency measures (syntactic), where accuracy is lowest.

    Words and sentences are counted with the tool's own rules (Concepts). describe_feature(key)["definitions"]["word"] names the word each feature uses.

    Tested versions: spaCy 3.8.16, en_core_web_sm 3.8.0, tr_core_news_md 1.0 (model version 3.4.2), Zeyrek 0.1.3.

    Split words¶

    When spaCy splits a word into several tokens, the tool takes the word's part of speech and morphological tags from the first token. For example, sosyo-ekonomik is an adjective but is counted as a noun. This only affects the features that use these tags; it does not touch the word, syllable and sentence counts or the readability formulas. In the newspaper columns, 0.65% of Turkish words and 2.70% of English words are split into more than one token.

    Turkish morphology depends on Zeyrek¶

    The 23 features in the morphological_zeyrek group and the Turkish lemmas come from Zeyrek. Zeyrek can return several analyses for a word; the tool takes the first without looking at the context (in the Kalyoncu formulas, the one matching spaCy's part of speech). To get the same numbers again, start Python with PYTHONHASHSEED=0.

    Two thirds of the candidates are still unverified¶

    Of the 172 verification candidates, 61 are done (61 ✅) and 111 are 🔍 open: most sources publish a formula but never a worked numerical example. Details: The verification system.

    Early development (0.x)¶

    Until 1.0, key names and the public API may change; changes are announced in the release notes.

    Things to know when using it¶

    These are not shortcomings of the tool: they are how the measures themselves behave, an input requirement, or an attribution rule. They still affect your results.

    Tool-specific features¶

    A few features are not literature measures but definitions this tool made. Their citations say so plainly:

    • punct_entropy, sent_len_entropy: Shannon's formula applied to the distribution of punctuation types and of sentence lengths. The formula is Shannon's; the decision to apply it there is ours.
    • polysyllabic_word_ratio: the ratio form of SMOG's input. Not a measure McLaughlin defines.

    There is nothing wrong with using them; cite the source of the formula, but do not present the measure as that source's own.

    Paragraph features depend on how the input is formatted¶

    The three paragraph features find paragraph boundaries from blank lines; a single line break does not count. If the text has no blank lines, the whole text counts as one paragraph; this is common in text extracted from PDF and EPUB. If a text longer than 1000 words has no paragraph boundary at all, a ParagraphStructureWarning is raised.

    What the syllable count reads aloud, and what it skips¶

    The readability formulas and the syllable features follow Çetinkaya's (2010, p.93) principle of counting syllables as they are read, so numbers, abbreviations and symbols are counted as they are read aloud: 1918 → bin dokuz yüz on sekiz (7 syllables), cm → santimetre, %50 → yüzde elli, 3. kat → üçüncü kat, 10:30 → on otuz, 3kg → üç kilogram, TBMM → te-be-me-me.

    In Turkish, a dot after a number marks an ordinal when a word or a comma follows it (3. kat); otherwise it ends the sentence (Sonuç 3. → üç).

    Forms whose reading cannot be told from the text are not guessed; they are left out of the syllable count: a unit letter on its own (m may be metre or minute), symbols whose reading depends on context (/, #, *), and unlisted lowercase abbreviations without vowels. In text dense with symbols and abbreviations, the syllable count therefore comes out slightly lower than a hand count.

    Phonological measures are computed from letters¶

    The measures in the phonological group (vowel shares, vowel harmony) are computed from the letters of the written text, not from phonemes; classes such as front and back vowels are letter lists. They do not work with IPA transcription; that is left for later versions.

    Heaps β is unreliable on short texts¶

    heaps_beta is measured at 20 equally spaced points through the text (Tweedie & Baayen 1998, p.333), so every text is measured with the same number of points. There is no minimum length; the value is computed for every text of 20 words or more, and a text shorter than 20 words gives nan. The law fits large collections well and is not reliable on short texts (Manning et al. 2008, p.81).

    The Kalyoncu formulas approximate hand counts¶

    The kalyoncu_* keys reproduce Kalyoncu's (2025) hand counts approximately, with Zeyrek and spaCy. Four of Kalyoncu's nine rules (common derivations, onomatopoeia, intensified forms, rare senses) and Kalyoncu's meaning-based sentence definition are not applied. KL rests on expert judgement (rules 3, 5, 8), so it is not expected to match the expert values exactly. Numbers written in words count as in the list; a capitalised word not at the start of a sentence counts as a proper noun; stems and verbals are counted from the Zeyrek analysis that matches spaCy's class (these three rules apply in the kalyoncu_* keys only). Verbal suffixes Zeyrek does not know and errors in the selected analysis change the result.
